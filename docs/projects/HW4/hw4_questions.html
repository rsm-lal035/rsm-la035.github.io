MaxDiff Analysis of MSBA Class Preferences

Counts, MLE, and Bayesian estimation of the MNL model

Author

Lawrence Lin

Published

May 27, 2026

Introduction

MaxDiff, also called Best-Worst Scaling, is a survey method for measuring relative preferences. Instead of asking respondents to rate each item on a numeric scale, the survey repeatedly shows a small set of items and asks for two choices: the most-preferred item and the least-preferred item. In this survey, each valid MaxDiff screen shows 4 MSBA classes.

MaxDiff is useful because people are generally better at making trade-offs than at calibrating rating scales. For example, two students may both give several classes a 7 out of 10, but those 7s may not mean the same thing. A best-worst task forces differentiation: when four classes appear together, the respondent must decide which one is strongest and which one is weakest. This reduces scale-use bias and gives us information about preference ordering.

In this assignment, the items are 10 core MSBA classes at UCSD Rady. I estimate relative preference using three approaches. First, I compute a simple counts score: the percentage of times each class is chosen best minus the percentage of times it is chosen worst. Second, I estimate a multinomial logit (MNL) model by maximum likelihood. Third, I estimate the same MNL model using a Bayesian random-walk Metropolis-Hastings sampler.

I expect the three methods to agree on the broad story, especially at the top and bottom of the ranking. They should differ most in the middle, where classes have similar preference strength and small changes in estimation method can flip the order. The MNL and Bayesian methods also give a more formal way to quantify uncertainty than the simple counts score.

Counts Analysis

The first method is a simple counts analysis. For each class, I calculate how often it was selected as the most-preferred class when it appeared, and how often it was selected as the least-preferred class when it appeared. The counts score is the difference between these two percentages:

\[ \text{score}_j = \%\text{best}_j - \%\text{worst}_j \]

A score close to +1 means that a class is almost always chosen as the best option when shown. A score close to -1 means that it is almost always chosen as the worst option when shown. A score near 0 means that the class is either middle-of-the-pack or receives mixed reactions from respondents.

From MaxDiff Data to MNL Choices

The MaxDiff responses can be treated as multinomial logit choice data. In each task, a respondent sees four classes and makes two choices. First, they choose the class they like the most from the full set of four. Then, after the best class is removed, they choose the class they like the least from the remaining three.

For the best-choice part, the MNL model assigns each item \(j\) a utility coefficient \(\beta_j\). If the task shows items \(\{a,b,c,d\}\), the probability that item \(b\) is chosen as best is:

\[ P(\text{best}=b) = \frac{\exp(\beta_b)} {\exp(\beta_a)+\exp(\beta_b)+\exp(\beta_c)+\exp(\beta_d)} \]

For the worst-choice part, I use the same utilities but flip the sign. Intuitively, an item with high positive utility should be unlikely to be selected as worst, while an item with low utility should be more likely to be selected as worst. If item \(b\) was already selected as best, then the probability that item \(c\) is selected as worst from the remaining items is:

\[ P(\text{worst}=c \mid \text{best}=b) = \frac{\exp(-\beta_c)} {\exp(-\beta_a)+\exp(-\beta_c)+\exp(-\beta_d)} \]

Therefore, each MaxDiff task contributes two MNL choice observations: one best-choice observation from four alternatives, and one worst-choice observation from the remaining three alternatives.

The model also needs a normalization for identification. Adding the same constant to every \(\beta_j\) would not change any of the MNL probabilities, so the absolute level of utility is not identified. I set \(\beta_1 = 0\) and estimate the remaining nine coefficients, \(\beta_2,\ldots,\beta_{10}\), relative to item 1.

After reshaping, the 680 valid MaxDiff tasks become 1,360 MNL choice observations: one best-choice observation and one worst-choice observation for each task. This task-level structure will be used in the likelihood function in the next section.

MNL via Maximum Likelihood

Next, I estimate the MNL model by maximum likelihood. Each item has a utility coefficient \(\beta_j\), with item 1 used as the reference category. Therefore, I set \(\beta_1 = 0\) and estimate the remaining nine coefficients.

For each MaxDiff task, the log-likelihood combines the probability of the best choice and the probability of the worst choice. The log-likelihood is:

\[ \ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{\text{tasks}} \left[ \beta_{j_b^*} - \log \sum_{j \in \text{shown}} \exp(\beta_j) - \beta_{j_w^*} - \log \sum_{j \in \text{shown} \setminus \{j_b^*\}} \exp(-\beta_j) \right] \]

where \(j_b^*\) is the item chosen as best and \(j_w^*\) is the item chosen as worst. I maximize this log-likelihood using BFGS. For interpretation, I also convert the utility estimates into shares of preference:

\[ \hat{s}_j = \frac{\exp(\hat{\beta}_j)} {\sum_{k=1}^{10} \exp(\hat{\beta}_k)} \]

These shares can be interpreted as the model-implied probability that each class would be chosen if all 10 classes appeared together on one screen.

MNL via Bayesian Estimation

I next estimate the same MNL model using Bayesian methods. The Bayesian model combines the likelihood from the previous section with a prior distribution over the utility coefficients. The posterior distribution is:

\[ \pi(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \propto L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \cdot \pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \]

I use a weakly informative normal prior on the nine free coefficients:

\[ \beta_2, \ldots, \beta_{10} \sim N(0, 10) \]

The prior standard deviation is \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16\), which is fairly wide on the logit utility scale. As before, item 1 is the reference item, so \(\beta_1 = 0\). I sample from the posterior using a random-walk Metropolis-Hastings algorithm.

I first tune the proposal step size with short pilot chains. The goal is an acceptance rate around 25% to 40%, which usually gives a reasonable balance between moving too slowly and rejecting too many proposals.

Comparing the Three Methods

The three methods give three different views of the same MaxDiff preference data. The counts analysis is the simplest descriptive summary. The MLE MNL model estimates utilities and shares of preference using an explicit choice model. The Bayesian MNL model uses the same likelihood as the MLE model, but summarizes a full posterior distribution, which makes uncertainty easier to communicate.

Discussion

Overall, the three methods tell a consistent story about MSBA class preferences. The strongest result is that MGTA 495 Marketing Analytics is the most preferred class in the survey. It has the highest simple counts score and also receives the largest share of preference in the MNL-based models. The next strongest classes are generally MGTA 453 Business Analytics and MGTA 455 Customer Analytics, suggesting that students are especially interested in courses that connect analytics directly to business and marketing decision-making.At the other end of the ranking, MGTA 444 Analytics Consulting is the least preferred class in the counts analysis and remains near the bottom in the MNL and Bayesian results. Other lower-ranked classes include MGTA 451 Marketing/Finance/Operations and MGTA 403 AI Math, Programming, & Analytics. This does not necessarily mean these classes are unimportant. Some may be more technical, more foundational, or less immediately attractive from a student-choice perspective. MaxDiff measures relative preference, not objective course value.Methodologically, the counts analysis is useful as a quick descriptive summary. It is easy to explain: for each class, we compare how often it was chosen as best versus how often it was chosen as worst. This makes counts analysis a good option for an executive dashboard or a first look at the data. However, it is less formal than the model-based approaches.The MLE MNL model is more rigorous because it models the actual choice process. Each task contributes information not only through the selected best and worst items, but also through the alternatives that were shown and not selected. The MNL results are helpful when we want formal utility estimates, standard errors, and shares of preference that can be compared across classes.The Bayesian MNL model gives very similar point estimates to the MLE model, which is reassuring. Its main advantage is uncertainty quantification. Because the Bayesian approach produces posterior draws, it is straightforward to transform every draw into shares of preference and then summarize uncertainty with credible intervals. This would be especially useful for simulations, such as predicting demand for a new class or estimating how preferences might change if the course menu changed.There are several caveats. The survey respondents are a self-selected sample of MSBA students, so the results may not generalize to all students or future cohorts. Preferences may also depend on prior coursework, career goals, current academic standing, instructor reputation, and how familiar students are with each course. In addition, the order and wording of class descriptions may affect how students interpret the choices.If I had more time, I would extend the model to estimate individual-level preferences rather than only aggregate preferences. A hierarchical Bayesian model would be useful because it could show whether different groups of students prefer different types of classes. I would also collect background information such as career interest, technical experience, and program stage to better explain why students prefer certain classes over others.
